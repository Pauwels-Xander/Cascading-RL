\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}
% ===========================================================================

\paragraph{Cascade model.}
We adopt the Motter--Lai load-redistribution model
\citep{motter2002cascade}.
Each node $i$ has load $\ell_i = k_i$ (its degree) and capacity
$c_i = (1+\alpha)\,k_i$, where $\alpha \geq 0$ is the capacity slack.
At each round, every node whose cumulative load exceeds its capacity fails,
and its load redistributes uniformly to its surviving neighbors.
Recovery actions restore failed nodes one at a time, up to a budget $B$ per
round.

\paragraph{Parameter grid.}
All evaluations sweep a $(\alpha,\pfail,B)$ grid defined as follows:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\alpha \in \{0.10,\;0.20,\;0.25,\;0.30\}$ — capacity slack,
        spanning low to high network resilience; $\alpha^{\dagger}=0.25$
        is the training value.
  \item $\pfail \in \{0.05,\;0.15,\;0.20,\;0.25\}$ — initial node
        failure probability, spanning near-intact to severely damaged
        networks; $\pfail^{\dagger}=0.20$ is the training value.
  \item $B \in \{1,\;2,\;3\}$ — reference repair budget per round,
        scaled linearly with graph size relative to $\nstar=40$ nodes;
        $B^{\dagger}=2$ is the training value.
\end{itemize}
The training cell $(\alpha^{\dagger},\pfail^{\dagger},B^{\dagger})$ is
explicitly included and flagged in all tables.
This yields $4\times 4\times 3 = 48$ parameter cells,
with 40 graphs $\times$ 5 seeds $= 200$ episodes per cell.

the key parameters.

All synthetic graphs use attachment parameter $m=2$, giving an average
degree $\langle k\rangle \approx 4$ across all three topology types.
Small BA graphs are the training distribution; Small ER, Small WS, Large
BA, and IEEE~300 are out-of-distribution settings testing topology,
structural, and scale generalisation respectively.

\paragraph{Policies.}
Six policies are compared throughout:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{RL}: the learned \textsc{dqn} policy evaluated greedily
        from the trained $Q$-network. \todo{results pending model finalization}
  \item \textbf{Greedy}: one-step lookahead — simulates every candidate
        repair and selects the action with the highest immediate connectivity
        gain.
  \item \textbf{Degree}: repairs the highest-degree failed node.
  \item \textbf{Betweenness}: repairs the failed node with the highest
        betweenness centrality, recomputed on the live graph each round.
  \item \textbf{Risk}: repairs the failed node with the highest
        load-to-capacity ratio $r_i = \ell_i / c_i$.
  \item \textbf{Random}: selects uniformly at random among failed nodes.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Metrics.}
The primary metric is \textbf{\ancfix}: the area under the
normalised-connectivity curve over a fixed time horizon, measuring both
the speed and completeness of recovery on a common scale in $[0,1]$.
Formally, letting $\mathrm{NC}(t)$ denote the fraction of nodes in the
largest connected component at time $t$ and $T$ the fixed horizon,
$\ancfix = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T} \mathrm{NC}(t)$.
Supporting metrics are:

\begin{itemize}
  \item $\ancadp$: \anc{} over an adaptive horizon that stops when the
        network is fully recovered or the budget is exhausted; rewards
        policies that solve the task quickly.
  \item \textbf{FinalNC}: normalised connectivity at episode end; captures
        the absolute recovery level regardless of speed.
  \item \textbf{Solved}: fraction of episodes in which full connectivity
        ($\mathrm{NC}=1$) is restored.
  \item \textbf{Rounds$^{*}$}: mean rounds to solve,
        \emph{conditioned on episodes that are solved}; omitted ($-$) when
        no episode is solved.
  \item \textbf{ActRank}: mean rank of the chosen action among all
        candidates (lower is better; rank 1 = greedy-optimal choice).
  \item \textbf{NCgain}: mean per-action normalised-connectivity gain,
        measuring raw action efficiency.
\end{itemize}

Statistical comparisons use Wilcoxon signed-rank tests \citep[p.\,75]{hollander2013nonparametric}
at $p < 0.05$ with bootstrapped 95\,\% confidence intervals
(1\,000 resamples).

\section{Heuristic Evaluation}
\label{sec:heuristics}
% ===========================================================================

We first characterise the cascade recovery problem through heuristic
policies, independently of any learned policy.
This serves three purposes: (i)~establishing a well-understood performance
baseline against which RL can be measured, (ii)~identifying the parameter
regimes where heuristics saturate and a learned policy is most needed, and
(iii)~determining whether topology modulates the relative merit of different
recovery strategies.

\paragraph{Why Greedy dominates.}
The \ancfix{} objective is a monotone submodular function of the repair
set: each additional repair helps, but with diminishing returns as the
graph becomes more connected.
\citet{nemhauser1978analysis} proved that for any monotone submodular
function, the greedy algorithm achieves at least a
$(1-1/e)\approx 0.63$ fraction of the combinatorial optimum, and
\citet{feige1998threshold} showed this bound is tight — no polynomial-time
algorithm can exceed it unless $\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}$.
Beyond the theoretical guarantee, Greedy is adaptive: it observes the
current cascade state (active failures, live components, candidate
bridges) at every step before acting.
Static centrality measures cannot access this information.
The resulting gap — $\approx\!0.07$--$0.10$ \ancfix{} over Degree on BA
and ER — quantifies the value of one-step lookahead.

\paragraph{Why Degree and Betweenness outperform Risk and Random.}
Under the Motter--Lai model \citep{motter2002cascade}, load is
proportional to degree, so high-degree nodes carry the most load and
serve as primary redistribution hubs.
\citet{albert2000error} showed that hub removal fragments scale-free
networks orders of magnitude faster than removal of random nodes; the
recovery analogue is immediate — restoring hubs reconnects the most
load-paths simultaneously.
This \emph{hub-first principle} explains the Degree advantage on BA
graphs.
Betweenness tracks degree closely on BA (Pearson $r\approx 0.7$--$0.9$;
\citealt{barthelemy2004betweenness}) because high-degree hubs also lie
on the most shortest paths \citep{girvan2002community}, so both
centralities identify the same repair targets.
\citet{holme2002attack} formalised this: on heterogeneous networks,
degree-targeted recovery is strictly more efficient than random, with the
efficiency gap increasing in $\kappa = \langle k^2\rangle/\langle
k\rangle$.

Risk selects by load-to-capacity ratio $r_i = \ell_i/c_i$.
Although motivated by cascade physics, this conflates
\emph{failure prevention} (stopping overloaded nodes from propagating)
with \emph{connectivity restoration} (reconnecting the largest component
as fast as possible).
\citet{crucitti2004model} showed that in degree-load models the nodes
accumulating the highest post-cascade stress are typically
\emph{intermediate-degree} nodes that absorbed redistributed load from
failed hubs, not the hubs themselves.
Repairing them yields low connectivity gain per action.
Random selection confirms by contrast that even the coarsest structural
signal is beneficial: \citet{cohen2000resilience} showed that
hub-targeted recovery restores macroscopic connectivity in $O(\log n)$
actions on scale-free networks, versus $O(n)$ for random recovery.

\paragraph{BA and ER: ordering preserved.}
On Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi graphs the degree distribution is Poisson with
variance $\mathrm{Var}(k) = \langle k\rangle$ \citep{erdos1960evolution}.
ER is more homogeneous than BA, yet retains a moderate right tail:
the hub-first principle applies in weakened form.
\citet{callaway2000network} showed that the efficiency of degree-targeted
versus random recovery scales with $\langle k^2\rangle$; on ER this
quantity is lower than on BA, narrowing but not eliminating the
Degree--Random gap.
The overall ranking $\text{Greedy} > \text{Degree} \approx
\text{Betweenness} \gg \text{Risk} > \text{Random}$ is preserved,
with uniformly lower absolute \ancfix{} on ER — a consequence of the
higher connectivity loss per failure in random graphs where no resilient
hub structure exists.

\paragraph{WS: Risk inverts the ordering over Degree and Betweenness.}
Watts--Strogatz graphs \citep{watts1998collective}, constructed from a
regular ring lattice with rewiring probability $p=0.1$, exhibit a
near-uniform degree distribution ($k_i \approx 2m$ for almost all nodes)
and high clustering coefficient
$C \approx \frac{3(m-1)}{2(2m-1)}(1-p)^3 \gg C_{\mathrm{ER}}$.
These two structural properties jointly explain the Risk anomaly.

\textit{Degree and Betweenness lose discriminative power.}
With $\mathrm{Var}(k)\approx 0$, there are no hubs: all nodes are
structurally near-equivalent, and degree-based prioritisation reduces to
near-random selection.
\citet{holme2002attack} showed that the efficiency advantage of
degree-targeted over random recovery vanishes on near-regular graphs.
Betweenness is similarly compressed: on a regular ring, shortest paths
are distributed uniformly, and rewiring at $p=0.1$ introduces only
modest betweenness heterogeneity \citep{watts1998collective}.

\textit{Cascade dynamics concentrate load locally, creating
Risk-detectable hotspots.}
When a node fails on a WS graph, its load must redistribute to neighbors.
Because WS has high clustering, neighbors of a failed node are themselves
densely interconnected, forming a tight local cluster.
Unlike BA, where excess load disperses through long-range hub connections,
on WS the load \emph{pools within the local neighborhood of the failure},
creating spatially localized overload hotspots.
\citet{buldyrev2010catastrophic} showed that in networks with high local
clustering, cascade propagation is governed by local neighborhood
structure rather than global centrality: failures spread within clusters
before jumping between them.
Risk, operating on the post-cascade load state, targets precisely the
nodes at the active cascade frontier — the locally overloaded nodes that,
if not repaired, will propagate the failure inward.
\citet{majdandzic2014spontaneous} showed that in clustered failure
systems, local boundary intervention is more effective than global
structural targeting, consistent with Risk outperforming Degree and
Betweenness on WS.
